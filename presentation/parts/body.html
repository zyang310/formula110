Foundation

What a controller is

the whole job, in one loop

A controller is a function. Numbers about the road go in; two numbers come out — how hard to push the pedal, how far to turn the wheel. Then it happens again, sixty times a second.

f : sensors → (throttle, steer)
  • The car has no map and no plan. It cannot think ahead to turn four; it can only answer “what now?” from what it senses this instant.
  • Ours answers with rules: if the road bends this much, aim this far off centre and hold this speed.

So a lap can only get faster two ways: a straighter path through the corners, or more speed carried through them.

Start here if anyone is unsure what we built. The car is not planning a lap; it is reacting, 60 times a second, to a few numbers about the road immediately ahead.

Approaches

Two ways to learn a lap

Aug 28–30 · split on Aug 28

Two ways to get a fast lap: memorize the answer for this track, or learn to react to whatever is ahead.

Lucas · alternative

Memorize the track

Offline, privileged cross-entropy search over open-loop throttle/steer sequences; then split the lap into sectors and learn the best behaviour for each.

  • Initial training reached about one lap in 30 s.
  • A multi-hour run exhausted memory and crashed the laptop.
  • A tape of actions fits one track and one starting pose.
Abandoned Aug 30
Zhi · primary

Tune a reflex

A hand-built reactive controller reads the road ahead every tick. Speeds, gains and distances set its behaviour; a CEM, then a genetic algorithm, tunes them across many random starts.

  • Works from any start: it never needs to know where it is.
  • The safe baseline was clean on 14 of 14 seeds before any tuning.
  • Every candidate is raced on 28–33 seeds.
Selected — everything after this builds on it

Seed — a starting point on the lap; the track never changes, only where you begin.·Cross-entropy search — keep a bell curve over the numbers and narrow it onto the winners.·Genetic algorithm — keep a crowd instead, and breed it.

Primary approach

How the controller drives

controllers.race_faster · v27

Sixty times a second it answers two questions: where on the road do I want to be, and how fast dare I go there?

1

Read the road

How sharply is the road bending 4, 9 and 16 metres ahead?

2

Choose a line

Start a corner on the outside edge, touch the inside edge at its middle, finish on the outside again. That bigger, gentler arc lets the car carry more speed.

3

Choose a speed

Aim for 25 m/s on straights and 14 m/s in corners. When it’s going too fast, it lets go of the accelerator and coasts rather than braking.

4

Drift like we did

By imitating our own recorded laps, we added a drift: once a lap it brakes hard into a corner to swing the car round.

116 numbers set all of this. Each round of searching tuned 1–20 of them.

Development strategy

Two loops: the laptop and the chat

Aug 26 – Sep 2

One loop is a computer trying thousands of small variations. The other is us deciding which variations are worth trying.

Inner loop · genetic algorithm

Race a batch of candidate settings from a few starting points → race the best dozen again from every starting point → keep that dozen, and breed the rest of the next batch by mixing two good parents and nudging a few values at random. About a minute per round, run from Zhi’s terminal: 771 rounds, ≈ 570,000 races, zero tokens.

Outer loop · a chat with an agent

Question → hypothesis → a new behaviour behind a default-off flag, with tests → launch a search → read the checkpoints → promote or reject → one row in the decision log. 20 conversations, 30 search versions, 52 logged decisions. Each chat is one turn of this loop.

Who did what
  • Zhi framed the questions, drove the human laps, launched every search and reviewed traces.
  • Lucas built and stress-tested the pre-training alternative and called its stop.
  • Together: split the approaches on Aug 28, compared results, kept lab notes.
  • Codex (and Claude Code) wrote the controller, search tooling, tests and traces, diagnosed bugs, and kept plan.md.

Version — one search run we set up: which parameters to search, how wide their ranges are, and how to score.·Generation — one round inside a version: the same parameters, new values.·Elite — the best dozen in a round, carried forward unchanged.·Crossover — a child takes each value from between its two parents’ values.

0:30 · 2 presses · leave by 2:30 — “The laptop tried 570,000 races. The chats decided what was worth trying: 30 versions, 52 logged decisions.”

If asked what a version is: one search run we set up. It picks which parameters are open to search, how wide each one’s range is, and how candidates are scored; everything else stays locked at the previous winner, and the search starts from that winner. Every generation inside the version searches those same parameters, only trying new values. Examples: v16 searched just the 2 start-speed-cap parameters; v19 re-searched corner speed and braking distance with wider ranges; v11 kept v10’s 16 parameters and only changed the score.

The key working agreement (decision log, Aug 28): the agent builds and verifies the tooling; we own the long-running commands. That kept tokens for thinking, not for babysitting a search.

Rules we adopted along the way: never rank on official seeds; stop a version after ten flat generations; reopen a bound the elites are pinned against before inventing a new lever; every new behaviour ships default-off so the incumbent stays bit-reproducible; never delete a losing branch.

Learning

What we learned — and the open question

where the 21 seconds came from

Two things you can improve: the rules the car follows, or the way you search for the numbers inside them.

  • Tuning numbers got us most of the way; the last big gains came from new moves, like coasting and drifting.
  • The search exploits any loophole in its score — it rewarded a car that crashed and then sprinted.
  • When the search stalls, change what the car can do, not how long you search.
  • Our best idea came from driving the car ourselves.
