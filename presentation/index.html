The 7.19‑second lap

How a reactive controller, a genetic algorithm, two AI agents and ten seconds of human driving took our car from a single 28-second lap to a 7.19 s best lap.

The problem

One function, called sixty times a second

Aug 26–27 · reading the repo
f : sensors → (throttle, steer)
  • Camera: where the centre line sits 4, 9 and 16 m ahead — lateral offsets c0, c1, c2 — plus heading error.
  • LiDAR: distances to walls and cars. IMU: yaw rate. Odometry: speed.
  • Output: throttle ∈ [−1, 1] and steer ∈ [−1, 1], every 1/60 s.

Score: survive 30 s, then laps, then speed. Every seed spawns the car somewhere different on the 181.1 m lap. CPU only, ≤ 512 MiB.

The controller never sees its position on the map. It sees geometry relative to itself: where the centre line is at three distances ahead, wall distances, speed, yaw rate.

That matters for everything later: a controller that reacts to local geometry works from any spawn point, which is exactly what random seeds test.

Approaches

Two ways to learn a lap

Aug 28–30 · split on Aug 28
Lucas · alternative

Memorize the track

Offline, privileged cross-entropy search over open-loop throttle/steer sequences; then split the lap into sectors and learn the best behaviour for each.

  • Initial training reached about one lap in 30 s.
  • A multi-hour run exhausted memory and crashed the laptop.
  • A tape of actions fits one track and one starting pose.
Abandoned Aug 30
Zhi · primary

Tune a reflex

A hand-built reactive controller reads the road ahead every tick. Speeds, gains and distances set its behaviour; a CEM, then a genetic algorithm, tunes them across many random starts.

  • Works from any start: it never needs to know where it is.
  • The safe baseline was clean on 14 of 14 seeds before any tuning.
  • Every candidate is raced on 28–33 seeds.
Selected — everything after this builds on it

Lucas: explain the tape idea. It optimizes actions directly, which is powerful on one fixed track, but memory grew with every position and the result would not transfer to a new start or track. That, plus the crash, is why we stopped it on Aug 30.

Zhi: the reflex idea. The policy is interpretable — every parameter is a speed, a distance, a gain — so search can tune it cheaply and we can read what it learned. Our very first plan (Aug 26) was imitation learning; we never built it, but the human recording returns later.

Primary approach

How the controller drives

controllers.race_faster · v27
1

Read the road

Turn the three camera offsets into the local curvature κ ahead — near and far.

2

Choose a line

Outside → inside → outside: a target up to 3.1 m off centre, blended by corner phase (entry, apex, exit).

3

Choose a speed

25.1 m/s on straights, 13.7 m/s in corners. Above target it coasts; it never brakes just to slow down.

4

Stay alive — plus three tricks

NORMAL → AVOID → RECOVER near walls. Event moves: a launch cap, a sweeper boost, and a 0.15 s brake-turn drift after the long corridor.

ControllerParameters has 116 named fields; each search version tuned 1–20 of them.

Walk the four stages in order — this is exactly what runs every tick. Point at the map: the dashed line is the centre line; the coloured line is our car, blue where slow, yellow where fast. The grey line is the baseline hugging the centre.

If asked "is this machine learning?": the structure is hand-designed; the numbers inside it were found by search against a score. That split — structure vs parameters — is the theme of our learning slide.

Evaluation

How we decided one controller beat another

fixed on Aug 28, never re-drawn

Our evaluator replays the Gradescope worker's tick order; a parity trial matched it on all 11 grading metrics.

Ranking — the first difference wins
  1. Survived all 30 s
  2. Completed a lap
  3. Inside the incident budget: ≤ 0.25 damage, ≤ 1.5 s wall contact
  4. Completed three laps, then fully clean
  5. Best lap — worst seed, then p90, median, mean, counted in whole 1/60 s ticks
  6. First lap, then distance
Promotion gates
  • 12 untouched validation seeds, all clean
  • 100 fresh soak seeds, zero eliminations
  • ≥ 12 of 20 role-swapped head-to-head races against minimum_viable

The optimizer only ever ranked candidates on training seeds (plus the two official seeds from v10, and the five live grading seeds from v26). Validation and soak seeds stayed untouched until a candidate asked to be promoted.

The ranking is lexicographic: safety tiers first, lap time only among equally safe candidates. We rewrote the lap-time part ten times — the surprises slide explains why.

Development strategy

Two loops: the laptop and the chat

Aug 26 – Sep 2
Inner loop · genetic algorithm

Sample 16–64 candidates → race each on 6–11 seeds → re-race the elites on 28–33 → cross over, mutate. About a minute per generation, run from Zhi's terminal: 771 generations, ≈ 570,000 races, zero tokens.

Outer loop · a chat with an agent

Question → hypothesis → a new behaviour behind a default-off flag, with tests → launch a search → read the checkpoints → promote or reject → one row in the decision log. 20 conversations, 30 search versions, 52 logged decisions. Each chat is a generation of this loop.

Who did what
  • Zhi framed the questions, drove the human laps, launched every search and reviewed traces.
  • Lucas built and stress-tested the pre-training alternative and called its stop.
  • Together: split the approaches on Aug 28, compared results, kept lab notes.
  • Codex (and Claude Code) wrote the controller, search tooling, tests and traces, diagnosed bugs, and kept plan.md.

The key working agreement (decision log, Aug 28): the agent builds and verifies the tooling; we own the long-running commands. That kept tokens for thinking, not for babysitting a search.

Rules we adopted along the way: never rank on official seeds; stop a version after ten flat generations; reopen a bound the elites are pinned against before inventing a new lever; every new behaviour ships default-off so the incumbent stays bit-reproducible; never delete a losing branch.

Evidence · inside the search

Why we swapped CEM for a genetic algorithm

real populations from our checkpoints
Cross-entropy method · v2 gate, 10 generations

The sampling distribution shrinks every generation — σ fell 7× in ten — and cannot grow back. It settled on a floor with the racing line almost off (entry offset 0.055).

Genetic algorithm · v19, 16 generations

A population keeps its spread. We reopened two bounds v13's elites were pinned against, and the population walked out of the old box: the winner lives outside it.

Left: CEM keeps a Gaussian over parameters and refits it to the elites. Watch the ellipse collapse — once it is narrow it can't explore again.

Right: the GA keeps 48 individual candidates, crosses and mutates them. The dashed box is where v13 allowed these two genes; its elites sat on the box's edge, which told us the box — not the controller — was the limit. Reopened in v19, the winner landed at 13.74 m/s and 2.04 m.

Failure that changed our approach

The optimizer was right. Our curvature was wrong.

Aug 28 · the CEM gate
c/d ≈ d/2R

What we used: offset divided by distance. It grows with d in every corner, so the far point always looked sharpest.

κ ≈ Δslope / Δd = 1/R

The fix: difference the segment slopes. Same answer at every distance — and the car's own pose cancels out.

The broken signal put 84.6% of corner weight on “entry” and 7.8% on “apex”. The CEM responded rationally — it switched the racing line off. We fixed the sensor model, not the search.

For a turn of radius R, the centre line's sideways offset at distance d grows like d²/2R. Dividing by d still leaves d/2R — it rises with distance even though the curve is constant.

Second differences kill both the constant (lateral offset) and the linear term (heading error), leaving 1/R. After the fix the GA's first ten generations went from 10 s to 9.4 s.

Learning

Optimize the training, or optimize the model?

where the 21 seconds came from

Each promoted version is classed by what was new in it. Structural versions were still tuned by the GA.

  • Search harvests slack; structure moves floors. Search alone took the safe car from 28 to 15.5 s. Below 7.9 s, 84% of the gain came from new behaviour.
  • The objective is the specification. Every loophole in our ranking was found and exploited.
  • Robustness is designed. Held-out seeds, default-off levers and clean promotion gates — never one lucky run.
  • Follow curiosity. Both biggest structural wins — coasting and drifting — began as a human “why?”.
If this isn't a model that is trained, is this just being brute-forced and reasoned via an agent loop?— Zhi, to Claude Code, Aug 31

This is our main learning. "Optimizing the training" — better optimizer, better objective, wider bounds — was worth a lot early and nothing at the plateau. "Optimizing the model" — adding a behaviour the policy could express — was what moved the floor.

The quote is honest: our controller is a hand-structured policy whose numbers were learned. A neural policy would learn the structure too, but would need far more data; that trade-off is the next slide's question.

Open question

Is Codex a more efficient learner than traditional ML?

three learners, three currencies
Genetic algorithm
570,000
30-second races · ≈ 198 days of simulated driving

Learned values for 1–20 numbers at a time. Refined every structure it was handed, then flat-lined at 457 ticks.

AI agents
504 M
tokens processed · 15.9 M fresh · 2.0 M written

Learned hypotheses: 30 search spaces, 10 rewrites of the ranking, one geometry bug, 12 new behaviours (3 rejected). Could not judge any idea without the simulator.

Human
10.4 s
of driving · 626 ticks · a handful of questions

Learned a prior: brake to rotate, never brake to slow. It seeded the 0.41 s drift — the largest late gain.

Our tentative answer: they learn in different spaces. The GA searches parameters, the agent searches structure and objectives, and the human supplies priors. Per sample the human wins; per hour, the agent; for the last hundredth, the GA. None reached 7.19 s alone.

Frame it as a question for the room, not a verdict. The units differ on purpose: races, tokens, seconds of driving. Note that 97% of the agent tokens were the conversation re-reading itself (cache), so "fresh" tokens are the fairer comparison.

Discussion prompt: what would it take for an agent to generate its own "human laps" — to play the game rather than read about it?
